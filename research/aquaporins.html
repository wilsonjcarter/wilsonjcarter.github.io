Four pores in one protein

Aquaporins sit in the cell membrane as tetramers: four identical subunits, each folded around its own narrow pore. Two short loops carrying the amino acids asparagine–proline–alanine (the NPA motifs) meet in the middle of each pore, and a tight constriction near the outside decides what fits through.

Most aquaporins pass only water. The aquaglyceroporins, including AQP3 and AQP7, also let through glycerol and, in the case of AQP3, hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), a molecule cells use as a signal but also have to keep in check.

A channel that closes itself

Cryo-EM structures of human AQP3 show the channel open at pH 8.0 and closed at acidic pH or after exposure to H2O2. Closing happens through a large rearrangement of loop E on the outside of the channel. Because H2O2 itself shuts the channel that carries it, AQP3 can limit its own traffic.

Blocking a pore from the inside

Aquaporins turn up in cancer: their levels track tumour growth, cell migration and patient survival, which makes them possible drug targets. Yet few compounds are known to block a specific human aquaporin. A cryo-EM structure of AQP7 with the inhibitor Z433927330, combined with molecular dynamics simulations, shows where it binds: on the inner, cytoplasmic side of each pore. Cancer cells treated with it grow more slowly.
