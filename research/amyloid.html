One protein, three regions

α-Synuclein is a small protein of 140 amino acids, abundant at nerve endings and the main component of the clumps found in the brains of people with Parkinson’s disease. On its own in solution it is disordered, but its sequence has three distinct parts, and each behaves differently.

Lowering the pH, as inside lysosomes, neutralises much of the acidic tail and removes some of the charge that keeps α-synuclein molecules apart. Acidic conditions are known to speed up its aggregation.

Chaperones that make things worse

Cells have chaperones to keep proteins like this in check, but they do not always help. The co-chaperone STIP1/HOP binds two motifs in α-synuclein’s acidic tail. Instead of preventing aggregation, it holds α-synuclein back from forming fibrils and pushes it towards amorphous, high-molecular-weight species that are more toxic to neurons.
