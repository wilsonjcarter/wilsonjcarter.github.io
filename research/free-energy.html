One proton, a different protein

Several amino acids carry a proton that comes and goes with pH. The pH at which a residue holds its proton half of the time is its pKa. Each residue type has its own typical value in water, from about 4 for aspartate and glutamate to about 10 for lysine and tyrosine. Inside a folded protein, the surroundings can shift that value by several units. A buried acid may stay protonated at neutral pH; a nearby charge can push the other way.

That one proton changes the charge of the protein, and with it stability, binding and, in aquaporins, whether the channel is open or closed.

Every unit of pKa shift is a free energy difference of 5.7 kJ/mol (1.4 kcal/mol) at room temperature. A prediction that is useful to within one pKa unit has to get the free energy right to within a few kJ/mol.

Two sulfurs, one bond

Cysteine carries a thiol group (–SH). When two cysteines meet and are oxidised, their sulfurs join in a disulfide bond, a covalent staple across the chain. Whether it forms depends on the cell: the cytosol is reducing and keeps cysteines apart, while the endoplasmic reticulum and the space outside cells are oxidising enough for disulfides to form.

A disulfide stabilises a folded protein mostly by what it takes away from the unfolded one. Pinning two points of a floppy chain together removes many of the shapes it could otherwise adopt, and the longer the loop between the cysteines, the bigger that loss. A protein that holds the bond without strain gains that free energy as stability, which in turn makes the bond harder to reduce.

Real disulfides are rarely that simple: the bond can strain the fold, and the protein can favour or resist it in ways a loop-length formula cannot see. Getting those numbers for a particular protein is another job for alchemical free energy calculations.

Computing it with alchemy

Simulating the proton actually leaving would need quantum chemistry and far more sampling than is practical. Alchemical methods take a shortcut: a parameter λ turns the residue from one form into the other inside the simulation, fading the proton into a non-interacting “dummy” atom and moving the charges to the deprotonated form. Doing this once in water and once in the protein closes a thermodynamic cycle:

waterAHΔGwater →A⁻ proteinAHΔGprotein →A⁻ ΔpKa = (ΔGprotein − ΔGwater) / RT ln 10

I use non-equilibrium switching, as implemented in pmx. Instead of moving λ slowly, many short simulations sweep it from 0 to 1 and back, each recording the work it took. Fast switching wastes some energy, so forward runs overshoot the free energy and reverse runs undershoot it. Crooks’ theorem says the true value sits where the two work distributions cross, and the Bennett acceptance ratio estimates it from both sets of runs.
